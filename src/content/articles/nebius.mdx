In 2024, Nebius was the spare parts of a Russian tech giant. Barely two years later, it's an AI powerhouse that helps the biggest companies on the planet deploy data centers.

So how did a company born out of sanctions land a two-billion-dollar investment from Nvidia, sign deals with Microsoft and Meta, and become a $60 billion AI juggernaut?

## Russia's Google

The story starts in Moscow in 1997, when the future Nebius founder unveiled a new product: Yandex.ru, a search engine that had indexed the entire Russian internet. It was the brainchild of Arkady Volozh and Ilya Segalovich, two friends from high school who had originally written the software to help search through Russian text, and named their product Yandex, short for "Yet Another iNDEXer."

Their edge was the Russian language itself. A single Russian word can take dozens of different forms, and most search engines, including Google, struggled with the nuances of such a complex language. The Yandex team used this as a moat, held off Google, and ultimately won the Russian search market.

Over the next two decades, Yandex became something closer to the entire Russian internet than a search engine. It didn't just do search, but also maps, email, music, ride-hailing, food delivery, e-commerce, and even an autonomous vehicle program. And it ran all of it on its own data centers, filled with servers it designed itself. Yandex had become a super app, and its 2011 listing on Nasdaq solidified its status as a bona fide tech giant.

On paper, the company was Dutch. Yandex reincorporated in the Netherlands in 2007, and its Nasdaq listing was the biggest internet IPO since Google's. The structure was built to attract Western capital, and it worked.

But the Kremlin had other plans. In 2009, two years before the listing, Yandex issued a single "golden share" to Sberbank, the state-controlled bank, for one euro. That one share gave its holder the power to block any investor from building a stake of more than 25%.

So while Western investors owned Yandex through the Nasdaq, Moscow treated it like national infrastructure.

## Half Price

Yandex's Russian ties would ultimately come to a head on February 24th, 2022, when Russia invaded Ukraine. Within days of the invasion, Nasdaq halted all trading in Yandex, and the stock would not trade again for the next 32 months.

The Dutch holding company that attracted so many Western investors didn't save them from the fallout. Yandex itself was never sanctioned, but Russia's capital controls prevented any money from being moved, and the Kremlin's veto meant they couldn't divest the assets either.

In June 2022, the EU added Yandex CEO Arkady Volozh to its sanctions list, forcing him to step down from the company and leave the board. Arkady would later speak out against Russia's invasion of Ukraine, calling it "barbaric," a statement that came at great personal and financial risk to himself.

### The Sale

On February 4th, 2024, Yandex agreed to sell its entire Russian business to a consortium of Russian buyers for about $5.2 billion. That may sound like a lot, but Moscow had a rule requiring any company from a country it deemed unfriendly to sell its Russian assets at a discount of at least 50%. The Netherlands was on that list, so the price was roughly half of what Yandex's Russian business was theoretically worth. The sale included almost everything people cared about: search, ads, taxis, maps and delivery. The entire Russian super app the team had spent decades building was gone.

But they didn't lose everything, and what remained would become the foundation of the company we know today.

After the divestiture, Yandex renamed itself Nebius. Volozh came back to lead the business once the EU removed him from its sanctions list, and the shares began trading again on the Nasdaq under a new ticker: NBIS.

But what exactly was left of the Dutch company?

## The Spare Parts

Nebius walked away with no debt, about two and a half billion dollars in cash from the sale, a data center in Finland, and more than a thousand employees, most of them engineers. From an outsider's perspective, it looked like a huge step down from the heights of Yandex. But it was actually the perfect setup for their pivot.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="the-split" />

From the day they relisted on Nasdaq, Volozh told investors Nebius would focus on one thing: AI infrastructure. His engineers had spent two decades running some of the most demanding computing workloads in Russia, powering search, maps and recommendations for tens of millions of people, in data centers they designed and operated themselves. They had a skill set the AI industry desperately needed: they could turn buildings full of chips into computing power that people could actually use.

Their timing couldn't have been better. Since 2023, demand for Nvidia's GPUs had run so far ahead of supply that even the biggest cloud providers were putting customers on waiting lists.

Nebius moved fast. They tripled the size of their Finnish data center, from about 25 megawatts to 75, and started filling it with GPUs. And in December 2024, Nebius raised an additional $700 million at $21 a share. The investors included notable firms like Accel and Orbis, but one strategic investor mattered more than the rest: Nvidia.

## Enter Nvidia

On paper, Nvidia's check was small, just about $25 million. But the size of the check wasn't the point. Nvidia's chips were the single scarcest resource in AI, and Nvidia effectively decides who gets them, and when. When Nvidia invests in a customer, it sends a message to the entire industry: this company is going to be at the front of the line for our chips.

And Nvidia wasn't done. Fifteen months later, in March 2026, it invested another $2 billion directly into Nebius. The deal came with early access to Nvidia's next generation of chips, Vera Rubin. By July, Nvidia disclosed that it owned 9.3% of the company.

The March deal also came with a partnership to build more than 5 gigawatts of Nvidia-powered capacity by 2030. And when Nvidia's stake was disclosed in July, Nebius's stock rose nearly 19% in a single day.

Nebius wasn't the only company chasing the opportunity. A whole new category of business was forming around the GPU shortage, and it had a name: the neocloud.

## What Is a Neocloud?

A neocloud is a cloud company built for one thing: AI.

Hyperscalers like Amazon, Microsoft and Google run the traditional cloud businesses that make up the bulk of the internet. They rent out computing power for almost everything, from websites to databases and storage. But when the AI boom hit, the hyperscalers couldn't build fast enough. The bottleneck wasn't just chips. It was land, electricity, and the time it takes to build a data center and plug it into the grid. Customers who needed thousands of GPUs online by a specific date were told to wait.

Neoclouds stepped into that gap. The business model is simple: secure land and power, build or lease a data center, fill it with Nvidia chips, and rent that computing power to AI companies, usually on multi-year contracts. The best-known neocloud, CoreWeave, pivoted from crypto mining to AI compute, and a wave of bitcoin miners followed.

However, not all neoclouds are built equal. AI compute is in such high demand that many deals are done as "bare metal." The provider supplies the data center and the chips, but the buyer has to figure out how to set up and manage the actual computing workloads themselves. This is common across the industry, especially among former bitcoin miners. The top neoclouds bring more than bare metal. They bring a platform, with the software and expertise to help customers run their AI infrastructure more effectively.

That's where Nebius comes in, and it's why one research firm ended up ranking it above Amazon, Google and Microsoft.

## 172 Ways to Fail

Nebius doesn't just rent out chips. It rents out the software and the expertise to actually get the most out of them.

That's immensely valuable for their clients, because running AI at scale is brutally complicated. It isn't as simple as pointing electricity at a room full of GPUs.

A single training run can spread across thousands of chips for weeks at a time. Every one of those chips has to be kept busy, which means scheduling work so nothing sits idle. The chips have to talk to each other constantly, so the network between them has to be fast and configured perfectly. Enormous amounts of data have to be fed in without creating a bottleneck. And things break all the time. Nvidia's own documentation lists 172 different ways one of its GPUs can fail. At this scale, something is always failing, and one bad chip can stall an entire job.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="172-ways-to-fail" />

Every hour a cluster spends idle or broken is money burned. So the real question for an AI company isn't just how many GPUs it's renting. It's also about utilization, and how much useful work it actually gets out of them.

That's the problem Nebius built its software to solve. Its cloud platform handles the scheduling, the storage and the networking, watches every machine for problems, and automatically pulls broken hardware out of service so jobs keep running.

On top of that sits Token Factory, Nebius's inference platform. Inference is the part of AI most people actually touch: every time someone asks a chatbot a question, a model runs on a GPU somewhere to answer it. Token Factory lets businesses run their models that way in production without touching any hardware at all, and production workloads on it more than tripled in the second quarter of 2026.

Nebius has also been buying its way deeper into the stack. In 2026 it spent more than $800 million acquiring Tavily and Eigen AI, and brought in the team and technology behind Clarifai. All of them point the same direction: making AI models run faster, cheaper and better on Nebius's platform.

Then there's the expertise. Nebius is consistently at the front of the line for Nvidia's newest hardware, and it has already received its first racks of Vera Rubin, Nvidia's next generation GPUs. That matters because every new generation of chips has to be learned. By the time most companies get their hands on new Nvidia hardware, Nebius's team has already been testing it, tuning it and figuring out how to run it in production. That makes Nebius less of a landlord and more of a partner who already knows how to get the most out of the newest chips.

According to SemiAnalysis, Nebius was the first provider to deploy Nvidia's HGX B300 systems, in December 2025.

When you put it all together, Nebius isn't selling raw capacity. It's selling a turnkey solution for AI computing: the chips, the software and the team, in one package. That makes it a premium provider in a market where many competitors are selling little more than space and power.

In September 2026, SemiAnalysis, a research firm that covers semiconductors and AI infrastructure, released its latest formal ranking of AI cloud providers. SemiAnalysis spent months stress-testing neocloud compute clusters and placed Nebius in its Platinum tier, the highest possible ranking, above hyperscalers like Amazon, Google and Microsoft. The only other company to share that tier was fellow neocloud CoreWeave. SemiAnalysis wrote that "the days of Nebius spooking prospects with their Russian accents are behind them," and described a robust offering serving a growing list of happy customers.

So Nebius was providing a premium product in a market starved for supply, and in September 2025, they'd announce a deal that changed everything.

## Microsoft Moves In

On September 8th, 2025, Microsoft, a company that spends tens of billions of dollars a year building its own data centers, agreed to rent AI computing power from Nebius.

The contract was worth $17.4 billion over five years, and up to $19.4 billion if Microsoft wanted more capacity. That capacity would come from a new Nebius data center in Vineland, New Jersey, filled with more than 100,000 of Nvidia's GB300 chips.

For perspective, Nebius's entire market value the day before the announcement was about $15 billion. The Microsoft contract was bigger than the entire market cap of the company, and Nebius saw a 50% run up in its stock price following the announcement of the deal.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="bigger-than-the-company" />

The deal was also structured to help pay for itself. Microsoft agreed to roughly $7 billion in upfront payments to finance the buildout, so not only was Microsoft renting the capacity from Nebius, they were also footing a large part of the bill to build it.

So why would Microsoft rent at all? The reality is that the bottleneck wasn't money or resources, it was time. Securing land and power and bringing a data center online takes years, and Nebius had the expertise and focus to deliver results fast. By mid-2026, Nebius had already delivered every tranche of capacity in the contract. But Microsoft wasn't the only tech giant short on capacity, and the next one would end up signing an even bigger deal.

Microsoft was no stranger to neoclouds. It was already CoreWeave's biggest customer. But this was a massive signal to the market that Nebius was a quality provider despite being so new.

## Then Came Meta

Two months after the Microsoft deal, in November 2025, Nebius announced a five-year deal with Meta worth nearly $3 billion. Volozh told shareholders they could've done a bigger deal, but they were limited by the capacity they had on hand. However, in March 2026 Meta came back for much more. They signed a second agreement worth up to an eye-watering $27 billion, nine times bigger than the agreement they'd penned less than six months earlier.

About $12 billion is a firm commitment: five years of dedicated capacity across multiple sites, built on Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips starting in early 2027. The other $15 billion is a safety net.

As a part of the deal, Nebius has the option to sell that compute to other customers and buyers in the market first. Whatever Nebius doesn't sell, Meta agreed to buy up the additional capacity. It's an indication of just how much demand exists in this AI compute space. For Nebius, it's a win-win. If demand stays hot, it sells the extra capacity to other customers, likely at higher prices. If demand cools, Meta picks up the tab, effectively underwriting Nebius's expansion for them.

By mid-2026, Nebius reported more than $40 billion in customer commitments, and investors had two key questions: will Nebius deliver, and will the revenue follow?

## The Run

The answer is yes, to both.

In 2024, Nebius brought in roughly $90 million in revenue. In 2025, it did about $530 million. In the first half of 2026 alone, it's done nearly $1 billion, more than ten times what it made in all of 2024.

When you look at ARR, or annualized run-rate, Nebius's growth is even more apparent. ARR takes the most recent month of revenue and multiplies it by twelve. As of June 2026, Nebius's ARR has reached $3 billion, up from $550 million nine months earlier, and management's target for the end of 2026 is between $7 and $9 billion.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="the-run" />

Nebius isn't just growing topline either, it's becoming more profitable. Nebius's AI cloud business posted an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 50% in Q2 2026, roughly double its margin from two quarters earlier. In plain terms, for every dollar of cloud revenue, Nebius now keeps about fifty cents before the cost of the chips and data centers themselves, twice what it kept six months earlier.

The team has executed well, but the demand for compute and increasing prices have been a huge tailwind for the business. Nebius measures its deals by how much revenue each megawatt of capacity brings in per year.

Megawatt based pricing is the standard in the industry, because the chips tend to change, but the power required to support them is more consistent to track.

Across its existing business in 2026, Nebius is getting about $12 million per megawatt, and for a sense of scale, the New Jersey site they deployed for Microsoft was 300 megawatts on its own. On the deals it signed in Q2, that number reached more than $20 million per megawatt. And on short-term deals, Nebius has found buyers willing to pay as much as $40 to $50 million per megawatt.

And prices are still climbing. On October 1st, Nebius raised its on-demand prices by around 20%, its second increase in three months.

Nebius also ran its first capacity auction, which cleared the highest price it has ever achieved for Nvidia's Blackwell chips.

When you reconcile that megawatt pricing with their plans for 2027 and beyond, the numbers start to get extreme. Nebius ended 2025 with about 170 megawatts in use, and it expects up to a gigawatt connected by the end of this year. Starting in 2027, Nebius is planning on adding an additional gigawatt of capacity every year. At $20 million per megawatt, every gigawatt adds about $20 billion in revenue a year, which means two gigawatts by the end of 2027 would pencil out to roughly $40 billion a year, if that pricing holds and they can build the data center capacity in time to service that power.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="a-megawatt-then-a-gigawatt" />

A common critique of Nebius's business is that it's highly reliant on Microsoft and Meta, and the numbers back that up. Nebius doesn't name its customers, but it does have to disclose any that make up more than 10% of revenue. In Q2 2026, three customers did, and together they accounted for nearly 60% of the business. Nebius doesn't say who they are, but the two biggest, at 24% and 21%, showed up right as the Microsoft and Meta deals came online.

It's even more concentrated on the contract side. Microsoft and Meta's deals add up to about $32 billion in firm commitments, roughly 80% of Nebius's $40 billion total.

But Nebius is already working on that. In Q2 alone, it signed four new deals averaging more than $1 billion each, with AI labs like Reflection and Cohere, another US AI lab, and a large US quant trading firm. Microsoft and Meta are still the anchors, but a second tier of billion-dollar customers is starting to form underneath them.

The value of contracts signed with brand-new customers grew more than 9x from the quarter before.

So the business is clearly scaling rapidly. But the economics of building capacity to support this growing business is where things start to get complicated for Nebius.

## Paying for It

Building AI data centers is extraordinarily expensive.

In 2026, Nebius expects to bring in $3 to $3.4 billion in revenue, but it expects to spend $20 to $25 billion on capital expenditures, mostly for GPUs and data centers. In Q2 alone, it spent about $5.7 billion building capacity against $582 million in revenue.

And Nebius is spending six to eight dollars for every dollar it brings in.

### Contracted vs. Recognized

You might ask yourself: how is that the case when they're signing deca-billion-dollar deals with Meta and Microsoft? The reality is that much of that is contracted revenue, and in order to start recognizing it, Nebius needs to deliver.

For example, when Microsoft signed for $17.4 billion, it was a promise to pay over five years, as long as Nebius delivers the capacity. That's contracted revenue, and it's what adds up to Nebius's $40 billion in commitments.

Some of that money shows up early, like the roughly $7 billion from Microsoft. But Nebius can't count that as revenue yet. It sits on the balance sheet as deferred revenue, essentially an IOU for computing power Nebius still owes.

Revenue only gets recognized as Nebius actually delivers, month by month, once each cluster is live. Spread over five years, Microsoft's deal works out to roughly $3.5 billion a year once it's fully running.

So the same deal shows up three different ways: as $17.4 billion in commitments, as about $7 billion of cash upfront, and as a steady stream of revenue over the next five years.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="one-deal-three-ways" />

That's also why the timing matters so much. Most of the deals Nebius signed in Q2 were signed against capacity that won't be ready until late 2026, so they won't contribute real revenue until 2027. The good news is that Nebius says those deals should pay back their costs in about one year and ten months, down from two to three years before. For infrastructure, that's fast. But the money still goes out the door before it comes back in.

One more nuance: the $40 billion is a number Nebius defines itself. CoreWeave, by comparison, reports a formal backlog figure in its filings, currently about $104 billion. Both point in the same direction, but they aren't measured the same way.

### The Burn

Put those pieces together and Nebius looks like a very different company depending on which number you look at.

On the income statement, it's losing money. Nebius lost about $190 million in Q2 2026.

On the cash flow statement, it looks flush. Nebius generated $4.5 billion in cash from operations in the first half of 2026. But almost all of that was customers paying in advance. Deferred revenue grew by $4.4 billion over the same period, which means that without those upfront payments, Nebius's day-to-day operations brought in only around $100 million of cash in six months.

Then there's the building. Nebius spent $8.1 billion on GPUs and data centers in the first half of 2026. Take that out of its operating cash and Nebius burned roughly $3.6 billion in six months, about $3.4 billion of it in Q2 alone.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="the-burn" />

And the bill is getting bigger, not smaller. To hit its full-year guidance of $20 to $25 billion in capex, Nebius will need to spend another $12 to $17 billion in the back half of 2026.

Now, that's all by design. Nebius is building capacity it has already sold, ahead of the revenue that capacity will generate. But it means the company has to keep raising money, and a lot of it.

### Where the Money Comes From

So how does Nebius pay for all of it? Mostly with other people's money.

The biggest source is its customers. Nebius expects more than $9 billion in customer prepayments in 2026, and about 70% of the deals it signed in Q2 included money upfront, covering 50 to 60% of the cost of the build. In a market this tight, customers are willing to pay for data centers before they exist.

The rest comes from investors. In the first half of 2026 alone, Nebius raised more than $9 billion: about $4.3 billion in convertible notes, which are loans that can later turn into stock, $2.8 billion from selling new shares, and $2 billion from Nvidia.

In August, it added another $5.75 billion in convertible notes. Because lenders are also betting on the share price, Nebius has been able to borrow this way at rates as low as 0.5%.

Going forward, Nebius is leaning more on traditional debt. In July, it took out its first secured loan, $775 million backed by its GPUs and a contract with an investment-grade customer, and with $40 billion in commitments to borrow against, management has said more is coming. It has also started letting partners build and own data centers to Nebius's designs, which lets it add capacity without paying for all of it.

Nebius ended June with about $8 billion in cash. With $12 to $17 billion still to spend this year, it will need every dollar it can get.

And despite the expensive buildout, Nebius's stock has absolutely crushed it.

## What It's Worth

When Nvidia invested in December 2024, Nebius shares cost $21. By June 2026, the stock hit nearly $300.

The ride hasn't been smooth. The stock bottomed at about $74 in February 2026, then quadrupled in less than five months. It peaked at $299.86 on June 22nd, the same day Nebius joined the Nasdaq-100.

In early October, the stock was trading around $220, giving Nebius a market value of roughly $60 billion.

To put that in context, Nebius's annual run-rate is $3 billion. So investors were paying roughly 20 times what the business was bringing in, which is a bet that the $40 billion in commitments turns into real revenue.

As I was recording this, in mid-October, the neocloud market fell off a cliff, on debt concerns at CoreWeave and other factors, so some of these numbers are a little stale. I'm planning a video on just the neocloud market. If you want it, drop a comment on the YouTube video and let me know.

Needless to say, not everyone agrees on what Nebius is worth.

In late September, Wall Street analysts' price targets ranged from about $84 on the low end to $410 on the high end.

And one of the most famous investors in the world is betting the whole thing falls apart.

## Fish in a Barrel

On August 6th, 2026, Michael Burry, the investor made famous by *The Big Short*, revealed on his Substack that he was shorting Nebius at $211.77 a share. He described betting against AI infrastructure companies as "shooting fish in a barrel."

A week later, Nebius reported its Q2 results, and the stock jumped more than 30%, leaving Burry underwater. But Burry has been early before. So it's worth a deeper look into the bear case for Nebius, and what the bulls say back.

<DashboardEmbed collection="nebius" slug="the-stock-and-the-short" />

The bear case comes down to three core pillars.

The first is chips. Meta's $12 billion commitment depends on Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips arriving on time in early 2027, and there's already been a hiccup. In September, research firm TrendForce reported that Rubin's ramp had slipped into next year because of problems with its memory chips.

But the chip risk may be overblown. Nebius has already received its first Vera Rubin systems and says the Meta build is on track. SemiAnalysis also reports that many providers expect to deliver Vera Rubin earlier than planned, not later.

The second is accounting. In 2026, Nebius started depreciating its servers over five years instead of four, which makes its profits look better on paper. If GPUs become obsolete faster than that, those margins are overstated. It's exactly the kind of accounting Burry has criticized across the AI industry.

But so far, older chips are holding their value. Nebius says prices for its older GPUs rose more than 30% in the second quarter.

The third is financing. Nebius's convertible notes are cheap as long as the stock holds up. But if the share price falls well below where those notes convert, around $180 a share for the notes it sold in March, and customers stop paying upfront at the same time, the next round of fundraising gets a lot more expensive.

For now, though, its customers are paying upfront. With prepayments covering about half the cost of the build on most of its new deals, Nebius isn't carrying the same risk as a company building entirely on borrowed money.

The bears raise two more points. One is concentration. Microsoft and Meta make up the bulk of Nebius's $40 billion in commitments. That's great while both are buying, but if either one pulls back, it leaves a very big hole. The other is construction. Building gigawatt-scale data centers depends on power companies, permits and construction crews, none of which move as fast as AI demand. Nebius's New Jersey site faced local pushback before it was approved, and SemiAnalysis says Nebius is dealing with delays at both Vineland and its site in Béthune, France. It has even told its clients that it thinks Nebius's year-end ARR target is likely too optimistic.

The bulls have more to point to as well. Demand isn't slowing down. Nebius has pointed to extreme demand again and again, and says it could sell its entire 2027 capacity today. Prices are still rising. The money comes back quickly. Nebius expects to earn back the cost of new capacity in less than two years, which is fast for any infrastructure business. It's spending heavily right now, but it isn't waiting a decade to see a return. Then there's Nvidia. Nvidia owned 9.3% of Nebius as of July and has effectively acted as a kingmaker, putting it at the front of the line for its newest GPUs. Nvidia's chips power nearly all of the world's most advanced AI training, and that edge isn't going away anytime soon.

### What Has to Be True

In the end, both sides are arguing about the same thing. For Nebius to keep working, AI compute has to stay scarce enough to keep prices high, customers have to keep paying upfront, and new chips have to arrive roughly on time. If those hold, Nebius's model compounds quickly. If any of them break, the gap between what it spends and what it earns becomes a major problem for the business.

## When the Shortage Ends

The next few months will tell us a lot. Nebius's Q3 results, expected in November, will show whether it's on track for that $7 to $9 billion ARR target, and the first Vera Rubin capacity for Meta is due in early 2027.

The bigger question is what happens when the shortage ends. Nebius's pricing, its prepayments and its valuation all rest on AI compute staying scarce. And the biggest buyers, like OpenAI and Anthropic, increasingly want to buy bare metal and run the software themselves. Nebius's bet is on everyone else: the startups, enterprises and research labs that need a working platform, not just a building full of chips.

Whichever way that goes, what Nebius has already pulled off is remarkable.

In 2024, Nebius was what was left over from a Russian fire sale: a data center in Finland, a thousand engineers and some cash. About two years later, in early October, that leftover company was worth roughly $60 billion.

It's arguably one of the greatest corporate spinoffs in history. Not because anyone planned it that way, but because a team built for one purpose happened to be set free at exactly the right moment.

The only question now is how long that moment lasts.
