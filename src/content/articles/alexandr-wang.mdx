Mark Zuckerberg made a massive bet, paying $14 billion to hire a 28-year-old named Alexandr Wang as his chief AI officer, and it's paying off. Meta went from a failing AI lab project to the number one free app in the App Store and an AI agent, Muse, available to the masses.

But what did it take to get here? Along the way, Meta spent billions, lost one of the most legendary names in AI, and had to go toe-to-toe with two of the most formidable labs in the industry.

## Nothing to Show for It

In July of 2024, Mark Zuckerberg wrote in a post on Meta's website, "Starting next year, we expect future Llama models to become the most advanced in the industry." Llama was Meta's flagship open model. Meta's original strategy was to build the leading open model, championed by its chief AI scientist, Turing Award winner Yann LeCun. Creating an open model was a big bet: by giving the model away for free, developers would build around it, make enhancements, and never be locked into a rival's closed model.

And they were investing billions to make it happen. In 2025, Meta would spend about $70 billion on capex, fourth highest in big tech, behind only Amazon, Google and Microsoft.

But they didn't have much to show for it. When Llama 4 came out in April of 2025, it wasn't the most advanced in the industry. It wasn't even the best open model, beaten out by Chinese alternatives like DeepSeek. Zuck knew Meta needed to pivot, and decided to make a big bet on a very young founder.

## What $14 Billion Bought

On June 12th, 2025, a company called Scale AI announced "a significant new investment from Meta" that valued it at "over $29 billion." Meta had invested roughly $14 billion for 49% of the business, but as a part of the deal Scale's founder and CEO, Alexandr Wang, would step down to join Meta.

In its next quarterly report, Meta described the deal as "acquiring a non-voting minority" of Scale, and booked $13.79 billion of what it called "the total consideration" as an investment. By the end of the year, the stake sat on Meta's books at $13.80 billion. A non-voting minority simply means Meta owns a big piece of Scale without voting shares, or as Meta put it, it does "not have significant influence over Scale AI's operations." Meta hasn't said what the rest of the money paid for, but the bulk of it wasn't a signing bonus. Scale said it would pay the proceeds out to its shareholders and vested equity holders.

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="what-14-billion-bought" />

So why did Meta want him? AI models learn from examples, and Scale's business was producing and labeling the data that AI labs train on. Wang had started it in 2016 as a 19-year-old MIT student, and at first it labeled street photos for self-driving cars. He dropped out, went through Y Combinator, and by 2025 he was supplying the same labs Meta was losing to.

Wang grew up in Los Alamos, New Mexico, the son of weapons physicists, in the town where the United States built its first atomic bomb.

On June 30th, Zuckerberg pulled Meta's AI teams into a new unit, Meta Superintelligence Labs, with Wang as the company's first-ever Chief AI Officer. This move was widely seen as sidelining Yann LeCun, who would end up leaving Meta to start his own company.

But Meta was also on a hiring spree for AI researchers, and the numbers they were offering got eye watering. Sam Altman claimed Meta was offering OpenAI's researchers signing bonuses of up to $100 million to join them. And according to the Wall Street Journal, one researcher turned down an offer worth about $1.5 billion over at least six years. Meta called that description of the offer "inaccurate and ridiculous."

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="the-offer" />

But by October, Axios reported, Meta was cutting about 600 roles from the superintelligence lab, and Wang told staff in a memo that "fewer conversations will be required to make a decision."

Some of the new hires didn't last the summer. Wired reported that three recent recruits had already left by late August, and Meta answered that "some people will decide to stay in their current job rather than starting a new one."

Back at Scale, two days after the deal was announced, Reuters reported that Google, which had planned to pay Scale about $200 million that year, was talking to Scale's competitors and planning to cut ties. Selling data to every AI lab gets a lot harder when one of them owns nearly half of you. A month later, Scale laid off 14% of its staff, and it ran under an interim CEO, Jason Droege, until Francis deSouza took over this August.

Scale has kept going on its own. It says 2025 was "our biggest year of revenue ever, driven by over $1B in new business," without giving a revenue figure, and in May the ceiling on its Pentagon ordering agreement rose to $500 million. That is a ceiling on what the Pentagon can order, not money committed.

So Meta had its lab, its leader and what was left of its hires. But to build what came next, Zuckerberg would have to rethink the thing Meta's AI was best known for. Llama was always open-weight, which means anyone could download the model and run it themselves. Back on July 30th, Zuckerberg published a letter declaring that "developing superintelligence is now in sight," and warned that Meta would have to be "careful about what we choose to open source." That line was a preview of what Wang's team was going to bring to market next.

## Avocado

Inside Meta, Wang's team was building a model code-named Avocado.

It was supposed to ship in March of 2026. It didn't. According to The New York Times, internal tests showed it falling short of the leading models from Google, OpenAI and Anthropic, and Meta's leadership even discussed temporarily licensing Google's Gemini.

Then on April 8th, Meta finally released it as Muse Spark. Axios reported it had been "built over the past nine months by a team led by Alexandr Wang," and Meta called it "the first product of a ground-up overhaul of our AI efforts."

And unlike Llama, you couldn't download it. Meta said it hopes "to open-source future versions of the model," but the company that had made open-weight models a central part of its AI strategy kept this one closed.

On the April 2026 edition of the Artificial Analysis index, Meta's last Llama model had scored an 18, but the new Muse Spark model scored a 52, a massive 3x jump. It still left Spark behind Google, OpenAI and Anthropic's flagship models, but it was progress, and promising to say the least.

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="from-18-to-52" />

In the same launch post, Meta said it would keep investing in "areas with current performance gaps, such as long-horizon agentic systems and coding workflows." Long-horizon agentic systems basically means AI that can carry out a long task on its own.

And Spark was already moving Meta's numbers. On the July 2026 earnings call, Zuckerberg said Meta had rebuilt its Meta AI assistant and integrated Muse Spark. And about six weeks later, they'd release an AI agent to the App Store for the general public.

Meta put Spark into its own apps and offered it to select partners in a private preview. On the July earnings call, Zuckerberg said he expects Meta to "get back to releasing some open source models at some point soon," and in May, Wang reportedly said on a podcast that Spark itself wasn't suitable for open sourcing. Meta's own evaluations were more flattering than the index. In its most intensive mode, which runs several agents at once, Meta says Spark scored 58% on Humanity's Last Exam and 38% on FrontierScience Research. Those are Meta's numbers, not an independent test. Meta kept shipping through the summer, with Spark 1.1 in July and Spark 1.3 on September 2nd, and it hasn't said which version was running Muse on launch day. In August, Meta did release a much smaller open-weight model, Muse Glimmer, and Zuckerberg said the weights for Spark 1.2 would follow "soon." As of early October, they hadn't.

## Free, Inside WhatsApp

On September 8th, Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent. Rather than the typical AI chatbot, Muse has its own computer and tools in the cloud so that it can take action on your behalf. Meta calls it the Muse Secure VM, short for virtual machine, which is a computer that lives in the cloud instead of on your phone or laptop. This allows Muse to act proactively and complete longer, more complex workflows than we've typically seen from your average AI chatbot. You talk to it just like you'd message a friend, in the Muse app or right inside of WhatsApp. And let's say you wanted to list a couch on Facebook Marketplace. You can just ping Muse with the photos and ask it to create the post, and it can open a browser and fill out the listing for you. Put simply, Muse can actually get stuff done for you in the cloud, across your email and the apps you use, making it a lot more useful than your typical consumer AI experience.

Meta says it can also fill out forms and negotiate on your behalf, and when it's time to pay, it can check out with a digital wallet from Stripe.

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="how-muse-works" />

Muse is on iPhone, Android and the web, and Meta says it's "coming soon to AI glasses." And around September 17th, Meta launched Muse for Mac, which works differently. The agent in the app works on its own computer in the cloud, separate from yours. The Mac app works on your own machine. As Wang put it, your agent can now get stuff done right on your computer, "files, messages, calendar, notes, all of it." Keep those two apart, because the Mac app would come back to bite Meta.

The real game changer for Muse is that it ships with a free tier. Wang told Axios that "for the vast majority of users, they should be able to do whatever they need to within the free tier" alone. Competitors' agents like SpaceXAI's Grok Bot and OpenAI's Dots each launched with paid plans only.

Muse is "free for most of what people need," with a usage limit Meta hasn't put a number on. If you want more, TechCrunch and Axios report two paid plans, Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100, though you won't find those prices on Meta's own pages. The one catch, according to TechCrunch, is that Muse still requires a payment card to get started.

So why give away something its rivals charge for? Because Meta has massive distribution. In June, an average of 3.6 billion people used at least one of Meta's apps every day across Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger. Many of those people are casual AI users, and Meta is betting that Muse can provide them with their first, and ideally last, taste of what a powerful AI agent can actually accomplish.

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="free-in-front-of-billions" />

As far back as April of 2025, Meta said almost a billion people a month were using Meta AI across those apps. For now, Muse is only in the US and Canada, and for anyone already on WhatsApp, there's no new app to learn.

So Meta had a free agent and a way to reach billions of people. But did people actually want to use it? The answer was yes.

## Number One

Muse took off fast. And two days after launch, Muse was already the number two app on the US App Store, with north of 83,000 iPhone downloads in the US, according to Sensor Tower estimates reported by TechCrunch. The next week, Sensor Tower had it passing ChatGPT as the top free iPhone app. By Monday, September 21st, estimates say it had more than 2.5 million downloads across iPhone and Android, and that day Meta's stock closed up more than 11%, at about $741 a share.

Sensor Tower's count was 730,000 downloads in Muse's first five days or so, and by the day OpenAI answered on September 29th, Semafor put it at more than 3 million.

The day after launch, Meta's stock rose about 6.5%. On Thursday, September 24th, it closed at about $778, which valued Meta at just under $2 trillion. It has since slipped back to about $728.

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="the-stock" />

When Meta reports its third quarter, expected later this month, I'll break down whatever it says about Muse the day it lands.

By the end of September, Axios was calling Muse "a personal agent that has gone viral and moved markets." And the face of all of it was Wang himself. He posted more than 100 times on X in the five days after launch, and by September 27th he was past 350 posts, most of them about Muse. Let's just say they didn't read like a corporate press office wrote them. Wall Street analyst Dan Ives even went on CNBC and called him "a special one." Most executives at a company that size leave all of the communications to the PR team, but Wang did it himself, to his audience of about 766,000 followers on X.

His posts included "muse for mac is HERE!!", and on September 25th, his essay "Why We're Building Muse." It opens, "Most people never say what they want." He asked his followers whether Muse could "make you $1000," under the hashtag #MuseMoneyChallenge, and Business Insider reported that many of the answers came from Meta employees.

None of this is new for him. In January of 2025, he took out a full-page ad in The Washington Post that opened, "Dear President Trump, America must win the AI War."

## Someone Else's Idea

In late September, Nat Friedman, who runs Meta's AI products alongside Wang, replied to a post on X: "We built Muse from scratch, but it is definitely heavily inspired as a product by OpenClaw." OpenClaw is a free, open-source personal agent created by a developer named Peter Steinberger. Reuters described Muse as "modeled on the open-source AI agent OpenClaw," and users found that Muse even carries a file called SOUL.md, with nearly the same contents as OpenClaw's.

And according to Steinberger himself, Zuckerberg had personally reached out to him. In February, Steinberger chose OpenAI instead.

So Muse didn't win on the model. Meta took someone else's open-source idea and built it, in its own words, "to work for billions of people."

Muse is a consumer app with a business footnote. Within three weeks of launch, Meta announced the Meta Enterprise Platform, with a Muse API and Muse Code, and Muse for Small Business the day after. And there is no evidence that Manus, the agent startup Meta bought in December of 2025, is inside Muse; China ordered that deal unwound in April.

But Meta wasn't the only company building an AI that runs your errands. Two rivals got there around the same time, and both of them are formidable to say the least.

## The Paid Rivals

The first was Grok Bot, from Elon Musk's SpaceXAI, and it actually beat Muse to market. It launched on August 11th, four weeks before Muse, as "AI teammates you can give real work to," and like Muse, each bot gets its own computer. But Grok Bot only came with paid plans, including SuperGrok and Cursor's Pro subscription and Teams plans, making it more of a product for AI natives than for the standard consumer.

SpaceXAI said Grok Bot had 418,000 weekly users as of September 14th.

In app-store revenue, Grok Bot got off to the stronger start. In each app's first eleven days, by Appfigures' estimate, Grok Bot earned about $175,000 and Muse about $27,000.

The second was OpenAI, which on September 29th, three weeks after Muse, launched Dots, always-on agents that Axios called "an answer to Meta's Muse." And the launch didn't go to plan. They did a live demo at OpenAI DevDay, and reports say it was unresponsive and the voice didn't work. Overall it was a bit of a bomb for ChatGPT, and they had to post a pre-recorded demo the next day.

On stage, the demo sat for what Futurism called "an agonizing ten seconds" without an answer, and NBC reported that the screen flashed "Voice chat couldn't start."

In fairness, it was only one demo. But unlike Muse, Dots isn't free either. It only comes with ChatGPT's Pro and Business Premium plans, which cost $100 a month or more, again putting it well outside the reach of non-AI-native consumers.

OpenAI had other problems that week. The day before Dots, it said it was delaying its next model, GPT-6.1 Astra, after internal testing showed it didn't meet its safety standards.

So compared to Grok Bot and Dots, Meta's Muse is the most accessible, the people's personal agent. But Muse was not without controversy.

## The Bill

Start with what we don't know. Meta still hasn't disclosed a single Muse user, subscriber or dollar of revenue. And one of the app trackers, Appfigures, put it plainly: "curiosity isn't necessarily the same as usage."

Muse ran into privacy concerns from day one. Reuters reported that Meta launched Muse despite internal concerns that the technology "mismanages its access to sensitive personal data." One Mac user wrote online that Muse had synced their Messages database without approval. Meta's spokesman, Andy Stone, said that Muse never accesses your full computer disk or messages without explicit approval first, but Meta also had to ship a quick fix for a security hole in the Mac app shortly after.

Stone says both Full Disk Access and the Messages connector have to be switched on first. Apple later said it would change macOS to make it more obvious when an AI agent asks for all the data on a Mac, which Reuters tied to the Muse complaints.

CNBC reported that Amazon has blocked Muse from its shopping site, citing privacy and security risks.

Then there are the people. In May, Meta cut about 8,000 jobs across the whole company, with $1.18 billion in severance. And according to reports, on September 9th, the day after Muse launched, Andrew Tulloch, the researcher who'd turned down that reported $1.5 billion offer and then returned to Meta anyway, resigned.

And some of the doubts are about Wang himself. In January, Yann LeCun said of him, "He learns fast, he knows what he doesn't know... There's no experience with research." In March, Meta's spokesman said Wang still runs the superintelligence lab and has "growing not waning influence."

Meta's continuing to invest in AI extremely aggressively, and it expects to spend $130 to $145 billion on capex this year alone. Meta's second-quarter revenue was about $60.8 billion, and after paying its costs and $31 billion of capex for the quarter, it was left with just $784 million in free cash flow.

<DashboardEmbed collection="alexandr-wang" slug="the-bill" />

Meta's core business is doing fine: second-quarter revenue was up 28% from a year earlier. For scale, Meta's capital expenditures in the second quarter alone were $31.08 billion, and it earned $60.46 billion of net income in all of 2025.

So it's clear Zuck is playing to win on AI and investing heavily. And that $14 billion bet on a 28-year-old is looking like a pretty good deal, all things considered.

So the $14 billion check that made all the headlines is about a tenth of what Meta expects to spend on infrastructure this year. The real money is going into data centers and servers, and Wang's job is to make that spending pay off.

Meta's third-quarter results will be the first real chance to see a Muse number from Meta itself instead of an app tracker. And as of early October, Muse Spark 1.3 ranks 23rd out of 224 models on the Artificial Analysis index, by its estimate.

The question now is whether Alexandr Wang can take Meta from the top of the App Store to the frontier of the AI race.
